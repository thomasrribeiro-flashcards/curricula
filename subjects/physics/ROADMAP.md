# Physics learning roadmap

## Learner and destination

- Long-term capabilities: model physical systems; connect theory, experiment,
  computation, and observation; solve and check unfamiliar problems; interpret
  uncertainty; and enter representative modern subfields without hidden
  prerequisites.
- Requested destination: `whole-field`. This is the current route, not a ceiling
  or a claim that every learner must follow every specialization.
- Focus branches: none specified; branch ordering is representative and awaits
  learner priorities.
- Deck granularity: course, with 6–14 estimated chapters per deck.
- Inbound knowledge policy: physics is unseen; catalogued mathematics and
  computing capabilities are required only where the graph names them.

The 2025 QAA benchmark and the IOP accreditation framework define the shared
undergraduate spine: classical mechanics, relativity, quantum physics,
electromagnetism, thermodynamics and statistical physics, waves, and properties
of matter, joined to mathematical modeling, experiment, computation, and
communication. AAPT guidance makes laboratory and computational work scaffolded
practices across the curriculum. APS units and NSF programs supply the breadth
check for contemporary branches. Full source roles, terms, and access dates are
recorded in `SUBJECT_BRIEF.md`.

## Sequencing rationale and coherence stress test

The graph has five layers:

1. `physical-reasoning-and-measurement` supplies observable quantities, units,
   systems, models, diagrams, estimation, and evidence language without using
   force, energy, fields, or waves as unexplained foundations.
2. Experimental and computational practice begin early and recur. The common
   undergraduate phenomenon spine then establishes classical mechanics, waves,
   thermal physics, electromagnetism, relativity, and quantum phenomena.
3. Upper-level theory decks deepen distinct representation grammars: phase
   space, continua, ensembles, vector fields, Hilbert-space methods, and
   research instrumentation. They are not bundled as an incoherent “advanced
   physics” deck.
4. Major subfields branch once their actual theoretical capabilities exist.
   Their table order is topological, not a prescribed ranking.
5. Four first-graduate specialization courses lead to one deliberately
   literature-facing research route. Additional research-specialization decks
   remain future extensions rather than inflated labels on survey courses.

Each proposed deck was checked against the 6–14 chapter range and four split
tests: conceptual spine, prerequisite set, representation grammar, and
culminating performance. Electricity/circuits and magnetism/induction are split
because the combined introductory E&M course would exceed a coherent deck and
hide the circuit-to-field progression. Quantum phenomena and formal quantum
mechanics are split so that evidence and simple models precede the linear
operator formalism. Thermodynamics and statistical mechanics are split at the
move from macroscopic/kinetic reasoning to ensembles. Introductory and advanced
experimental work are split at the transition from guided measurement to
open-ended instrumentation. AMO and laser/quantum optics are split at coherent
light-matter control and open-system methods.

Graduate-course evidence corrected four maturity labels without discarding
their sound scopes. Quantum information, accelerator physics, QFT, and
nonequilibrium physics are first graduate courses here: each establishes a new
formalism before asking for frontier reading. The accelerator route gained the
smallest missing hard prerequisite, `analytical-mechanics-and-nonlinear-dynamics`,
because beam phase space, transfer maps, Hamiltonian motion, nonlinear stability,
and collective effects may be assumed immediately. QFT keeps analytical mechanics
as recommended preparation because its opening chapter explicitly establishes
the action, classical-field, symmetry, and Noether grammar; requiring the entire
mechanics deck would be a false prerequisite. Likewise, the nonequilibrium deck
teaches Markov, master-equation, Langevin, and Fokker–Planck representations from
already-established probability and differential equations, so the catalogued
stochastic-processes deck remains recommended rather than hard.

No hard edge exists merely because a course is conventionally earlier. For
example, classical mechanics is only recommended before thermodynamics and
electricity; nuclear physics is recommended, not required, before particle
physics; and astrophysics is recommended, not required, before general
relativity. Conversely, waves are a true prerequisite for the quantum-phenomena
bridge, and vector calculus is a true prerequisite for upper-level
electromagnetic and continuum field methods.

## Cross-subject reuse and subject-specific transfer

The roadmap reuses only capabilities present in the supplied catalog. Algebra,
calculus, differential equations, vector calculus, probability, statistical
inference, mathematical computing, information theory, differential geometry,
and complex analysis are referenced as `mathematics/deck` rather than recreated.

Three local decks remain methods-oriented because the transfer itself is
physics content:

- `physical-reasoning-and-measurement` teaches system choice, physical
  quantities, units, scale, modeling assumptions, and physics-specific
  representation translation; it is not a duplicate algebra course.
- `experimental-physics-and-data` does not require the entire external
  statistics deck. Introductory uncertainty, calibration, residuals, and
  measurement decisions are taught in physical context; full statistical
  inference is useful preparation and therefore `recommended_after`.
- `computational-physics-and-modeling` requires the established mathematical
  computing capability, then teaches model-to-code translation, discretization,
  validation, numerical physical insight, and reproducible physics workflows.

No chemistry, biology, or Earth-science capability appears in the supplied
catalog, so none is invented. Their specialization decks must introduce only
the contextual system knowledge needed to apply established physics. If future
catalog entries supply a genuinely required broad capability, the corresponding
edge can be added after semantic review.

## Cross-deck concepts

- System boundary, frame, coordinates, sign, scale, approximation, and regime
  of validity recur in every modeling deck.
- Conservation, symmetry, equilibrium, stability, fluctuation, response, and
  causality deepen from concrete models to field and research theories.
- Energy, momentum, entropy, information, field, potential, state, observable,
  and measurement outcome require explicit interference contrasts at each new
  level.
- Representations recur with increasing maturity: system and experimental
  diagrams; graphs and residuals; phase and state spaces; fields and potentials;
  spectra and energy levels; spacetime and causal diagrams; reciprocal space;
  interaction diagrams; detector outputs; probability distributions; and
  simulation/data products.
- Evidence practices spiral from units and sanity checks through uncertainty,
  calibration, model comparison, validation, literature synthesis, and
  communication of limitations.

## Practice outside SRS

Every layer needs non-card work: long and mixed problems, derivations,
estimation, experiments with real measurements, apparatus construction and
troubleshooting, numerical projects, authentic-data analysis, oral defenses,
technical writing, collaboration, and literature reading. Before a deck is
built, its README should name the corresponding practice portfolio. The
undergraduate route should culminate in at least one open-ended investigation;
research routes require reproduction or critique of a published result and a
substantial project. SRS maintains component knowledge and method triggers but
does not certify laboratory safety, clinical competence, engineering practice,
or research independence.
